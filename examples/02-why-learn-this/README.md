# Why You Should Learn to Use an AI Agent First

> The overview for this whole course: where this lesson sits in the learning path, and where its boundaries are.

## 1. Overview

This is the first real piece in the series (the one before it is the series index, just a map). You don't need to do anything hands on here, just spend ten or fifteen minutes getting one thing straight in your head: why, before you learn anything else, you should install an AI Agent desktop app and start using it.

This piece doesn't teach you how to install it, that comes later. What this piece answers is "why does this come first" and "what does this course cover, and what doesn't it cover."

## 2. Learning Objectives

There's probably more you want to learn and more you want to get done than ever before. But here's what actually happens: you read a paragraph three times and still don't get it, you bookmark a resource and never open it again, you get two steps into something you wanted to build and then quietly drop it. That's not because you're not trying hard enough, it's because grinding through hard material alone is just naturally inefficient.

Having an Agent alongside you while you learn versus not having one isn't a matter of a little faster or a little slower. It decides whether you actually stick with the thing to the end. That's why this lesson has to come first in the whole path: it's the foundation, not an accessory.

By the end of this task, you'll be able to:

1. Explain why everything else you learn and do in this course rests on being able to use an AI Agent.
2. Look at anything you need to get done through the mini project lens, and know where Agent Skill fits into that picture.
3. State the boundaries of this course: which three tools it teaches, why only the Desktop versions, and why it skips Cursor style tools.

## 5. With an Agent and Without One, You're a Different Species

Let's say this up front: everything about learning in the rest of this series assumes you have an Agent by your side.

With one, your learning changes shape: if a passage doesn't make sense, you ask it to explain the same thing a different way until it clicks; if you think you understand something, you ask it to quiz you and find out whether you really do or just feel like you do; if you've collected a pile of scattered material, you have it digest that into notes you can actually get through; if you've built something, you have it point out the rough edges and help you fix them.

Without one, you're back to the old way: grinding through it alone, setting it aside when you hit a wall, and eventually giving up once it's been sitting long enough.

So this isn't a question of "a productivity tool would be nice to have." It's a question of whether you can keep learning at all. To put it bluntly: if you don't know how to use a Coding Agent, you won't be able to get anything else in this course done. That's exactly why we're spending an entire lesson clearing this hurdle first.

## 6. One Prerequisite You Can't Skip: Somewhere to Keep Your Output

Working with an Agent, you'll constantly be producing things: a document, a research writeup, reading notes, a snippet of code. You need somewhere to store that output, somewhere you can edit it, and somewhere you can look back and see exactly what you changed.

That place is a repo on GitHub. Get used to this pattern: one task equals one folder, that folder is a repo, and it's the container for that piece of work. Everything lives inside it, every change is tracked, and it travels with you if you switch computers.

So this course has one minimum requirement for you: you need to already know how to do these three things.

- Create a repo on GitHub.
- Clone that repo to your own computer.
- After making changes, commit, then push back to the cloud to save your work.

This course does not teach these three things. If you don't know how yet, go pick it up first and then come back, there are plenty of tutorials online and it's maybe half an hour of work. But please do pick it up first: every piece after this one assumes you already know it.

## 7. Treat Everything You Do as a Very Small Project

Here's the most important lens in this whole course, worth reading slowly.

Whether it's learning something new, reading a book, writing a report, building a small tool, or finally nailing down a concept that's been bugging you, these things look completely different on the surface, but they can all be handled the same way: it's a single task, it has clear boundaries, and it produces something.

So treat it as a project. And a very small one at that, small enough to fit in one folder, small enough that you can state what it's actually solving in a single sentence.

Why look at it this way? Because the moment you frame something as a small project, it stops being "a vague cloud of anxiety" and becomes "a bounded piece of work." Only once it has boundaries can you talk about handing it off to someone (or something) to do, and only then can you tell whether it's done.

This lens is the foundation for everything that follows. The sooner you get used to it, the smoother the rest of this course goes.

## 8. The Most Valuable Skill of This Era Is Building Experts

A lot of people assume that being good at AI means "knowing how to phrase your question." It doesn't.

What actually separates people is something else entirely: when you get a problem, instead of rushing to ask about it, you first classify it. What kind of problem is this? What kind of expert would handle this kind of problem? What should that expert know, what playbook should it follow, what should its output look like? Once you've worked that out, you build that expert.

In the Agent world, the thing you build has a name: **Agent Skill**.

The idea behind a Skill is simple: you can package up any capability an Agent has into a Skill. A script, sure. A prompt, sure. A persona, sure. A knowledge base, sure. Mixing all of those together, even better. Once it's packaged, the next time you hit the same kind of problem, you don't have to explain the whole backstory from scratch, you just call up that expert and put it to work.

So how big should a Skill's scope be? The answer is the same mini project from the last section: **one Skill covers one category of work, and its boundary is the boundary of that small project.** The two ideas are a matched set.

Agent Skill is the single thing you'll use most often going forward, hands down. But this course only aims to make you aware it exists, and once you've got your tools installed, walks you through building one extremely simple summarize skill by hand, so you get a real feel for what it's like. That's it. Going deeper than that would take several more courses on its own, not this one.

## 9. We Only Teach Three, and Only the Desktop Versions

When it comes to building foundation models, there are exactly three players in the world's first tier, and each one has staked out its own turf:

- **Anthropic**, whose model is Claude. It currently has the strongest reputation and adoption among US enterprises, the one businesses trust the most.
- **OpenAI**, whose model is GPT, and whose product is the ChatGPT everyone knows. It's the one used most by the general public.
- **Google**, whose model is Gemini. Backed by a tech giant with deep platform infrastructure, it came later but is moving fast.

On the desktop, the Agent tools that map to these three are exactly the ones this course teaches: **Claude Code, Codex, and Antigravity**.

Two boundaries worth stating up front. First, we only cover the **Desktop** versions, meaning the kind that installs on your computer, with windows and buttons, usable with a few clicks of the mouse. This course is for readers of any background, it doesn't require a tech industry background and it doesn't require you to become a computer expert. Second, you only need to **pick one of these three to learn**, not all three. How to pick based on your own situation is covered in Piece 04.

## 10. Why We Don't Teach Cursor and Its Peers

You've probably heard of products like Cursor, usually called Coding IDEs. Why doesn't this course mention them at all?

There's exactly one reason: **they don't come with their own model.** Products in this category don't build models themselves, they run on someone else's model. What they build is the shell around it.

And the deepest moat in this entire industry happens to be the model itself. The next piece will dig into this in full, but here's the short version for now: the model is the brain, the software around it is just the shell; there are only a handful of brains, but shells can stack up endlessly and get swapped out at any time. A product that's all shell with no brain of its own has a very thin moat, and it could be gone one day without warning. When that happens, all the time you spent learning it disappears along with it.

One possible misunderstanding to head off here: the test isn't "does it look like an IDE." Antigravity is itself an IDE and we still teach it. The test is **whether there's a brain of its own behind it**. Behind Antigravity sits Google's own Gemini, so it comes with a model. Behind Cursor there's no model of its own, so it doesn't.

We only study the three with the deepest moats, the ones building both their own models and their own Agents. Tuition, meaning your time, only goes toward things that aren't likely to vanish.

## 11. Exercise

### Exercise: Find Three Mini Projects of Your Own

**Goal:** Apply the lens from Section 7 to something real in your own life.

**How to do it:**

1. Grab a sheet of paper or open a blank document, and write down three things you genuinely want to learn or build lately. The more specific the better, don't write something like "learn Spanish."
2. Add one sentence to each: what it's meant to solve, and what you'll have in hand once it's done (a set of notes? a report? a small working tool?).
3. Check whether each of the three could fit inside a single folder.

**What you'll notice:**

Some things you can describe in one clean sentence. Others you'll find you genuinely can't explain what they're supposed to solve. The ones you can't pin down usually aren't hard, they're just too big, and need to be broken apart first.

> **Key insight:** Whether something can be framed as a small project is the precondition for whether it can be handed off to get done. If you can't explain what you want clearly, nobody can help you with it, not even AI.

## 12. Recap: What We Learned

- Everything you learn and do from here on assumes there's an Agent by your side. Having one versus not isn't about efficiency, it's about whether you can keep going. That's why this lesson comes first.
- You need somewhere to store your output, and that place is a repo on GitHub. Knowing how to create a repo, clone it, and commit and push is the one hard prerequisite for this course, and we don't teach it.
- Anything you're working on can be treated as a very small project: it has boundaries, it produces something, and it fits in one folder.
- The most valuable skill isn't asking good questions, it's classifying problems and then building the matching expert. In the Agent world that expert is called an Agent Skill, and its boundary is exactly the boundary of a mini project.
- This course only teaches the Desktop versions of Claude Code, Codex, and Antigravity, and you only need to pick one. It doesn't teach Cursor and similar tools, because they don't have a model of their own behind them.

## 13. A Note From Your Mentor

**Why this piece matters:**

Most people learning AI tools start with "step one of the tutorial: open the website." They install it, click around a bit, and then what? Then nothing, because they never actually worked out what place this thing occupies in their life.

The entire point of this piece is to get you to think that through before you touch anything. You're not just installing a piece of software, you're replacing the foundation of how you'll learn for years to come.

**Key insights:**

- Tools change, the lens doesn't. Which of the three tools is strongest today might be different a year from now; but the lens of "frame something as a small project, then build an expert for that category of work" will keep being useful.
- Your time is the most expensive cost you have. Spend it on things with deep moats, not on shells that could vanish at any moment.
