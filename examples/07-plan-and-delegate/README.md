# Plan, Delegate, and Act Like a Boss, the Mindset for Running Several Agents at Once

> By this point you have already gotten a feel for at least one Agent App. This task does not teach you any more buttons. It teaches you exactly one thing: how to go faster with the tools you already know.

## 1. Overview

The earlier tasks covered the big picture, how to pick a tool, and the hands-on details of one specific tool. You can now chat with AI, have it read and write files, and use an Agent Skill. But that only means you **know how to use it**, not that you **use it fast**.

This task is about what the people who somehow produce ten times the output of everyone else are actually doing differently. The answer has nothing to do with which tool they use. It has everything to do with how they organize their own head and their own time.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

Have you ever handed AI a task and then just sat there, staring at the screen, watching it think line by line and write line by line, while you do absolutely nothing but wait? That stretch of waiting is pure, unrecoverable waste.

That is not how the real power users operate. They keep several windows open at the same time, hand a different task to each one, and the moment one window is far enough along, they switch to the next, hand off the next task, and check the next batch of progress. This is what people mean by **the feeling of being a boss**: you are no longer the person with your head down grinding through one task. You are the person coordinating several things forward at once.

By the end of this task, you will be able to:

1. Explain why "sitting there watching AI think" is an efficiency black hole, and how running several sessions at once fixes it.
2. Build the habit of writing the problem you are about to tackle into a file before you start, so that even if a session gets lost or a long time passes, you can pick it right back up.
3. Tell apart "tasks that need deep exploration" from "tasks that only need review and sign-off," and use that distinction to decide what can run in parallel and what has to happen one at a time.

## 5. AI Takes Time to Work, So Do Not Zone Out While You Wait

You have definitely seen this scene before, maybe you have even been in it: you type, you hit enter, and then what? Then your eyes lock onto the screen, watching it print "reading file," watching the text crawl out line by line, one minute, three minutes, five minutes go by, and the entire time all you have done is stare. You think you are efficiently delegating work, but during those minutes your own output is exactly zero. You are just keeping the AI company while it works overtime.

This is not a flaw in the AI. Waiting itself is inherently negative-efficiency. An Agent needs real time to do real work: reading files, thinking through an approach, making the actual changes, running a check, all of that takes time, and all of it means waiting. While it is working as hard as it can, if all you are doing is staring at the screen, you are working just as hard at wasting time.

You have probably run into that claim floating around online, "one person doing the work of an entire team in a single day," and probably wondered how anyone actually pulls that off. The secret is often hiding in this one plain, unglamorous move: they never just sit there waiting for a single AI. Their screen has two, three, sometimes four or five windows open at the same time, each one grinding on something different. The moment they finish handing off the first task, they do not wait, they switch straight to the second window and hand off the next task; once that is handed off, they switch to a third. By the time they circle back to the first window, it has long since finished, and the result is sitting there waiting for them to review. These windows spin along like several production lines running at once, and you are the one walking back and forth between the lines, never waiting on any of them.

Strip away the details and this scene is simply **the feeling of being a boss**. A boss is never the person who parks themselves in front of one task from start to finish and grinds it out alone. A boss is the person holding several threads in their head at once, doing a lap every so often, nodding here, dropping a note of feedback there, then moving on to the next lap. You are not anyone's boss yet, but starting today, you can already start building this exact feeling for yourself, using AI as the practice ground.

That is the starting point for the two things this task wants to nail down: first, how to keep several things moving forward at once without losing track of any of them, and second, how to judge which things can genuinely move forward together.

## 6. A Bad Memory Loses to a Good Notebook, Write the Plan Before You Start

Let's talk about the first trap. You open three windows and hand off three different tasks. By the time you hand off the third one and circle back to check the first, you can no longer remember what you originally asked it to do, or what output you were even expecting. This is completely normal. It is not that your memory is bad.

**The human brain was never built to run in parallel.** No matter how sharp someone is, they cannot deeply think through several things at the same time. The only difference between people is that some have slightly better memory, which does help a little, but the far more reliable approach is to stop relying on memory at all and rely instead on **a good notebook**: make a plan before you start any piece of work.

Concretely, here is what that looks like: before you open a session, first get clear in your own head what problem you are actually trying to solve this round, and roughly how many rounds of back-and-forth it will take to get there. You can even treat the first few rounds of conversation as not "doing the work" at all, but instead as working with the AI to pin down this round's goal, scope, and steps, and then have it write all of that into a file. That way, even if the session gets lost, even if you do not come back to it for a long time, you do not have to rely on memory. One glance at the plan document, one scan of the chat history, and you instantly know what you originally set out to do and how far you had gotten.

This works exactly the same way as leading a team. The person running a small team of a dozen or so people never relies on memory either. They rely on writing things down, so that both they and their team can pick up right where they left off at any moment. What you are practicing here, in your dealings with AI, is precisely this same habit.

## 7. The Human Brain Cannot Parallelize, So First Figure Out Whether a Task Even Can

A good notebook beating a good memory solves the problem of "forgetting." But there is a more fundamental problem underneath it: **some tasks simply should not run in parallel in the first place.**

Tasks generally fall into two categories:

- **Exploratory tasks**: you have not yet figured out how to approach it, and you are not even sure the direction is right. This kind of work demands your full attention, going back and forth with the AI repeatedly until the direction becomes clear. This kind of work cannot be parallelized. Your brain can only deeply think through one of these at a time.
- **Execution and review tasks**: the direction is already clear, and what is left is following the steps one by one, while you just check whether each result is correct and whether to continue. This kind of work does not consume your deep thinking capacity, so you can keep an eye on several of these at once.

Before you hand off any task, ask yourself: **does this need me to figure out how to do it, or do I already know how, and it just needs someone to go do it?** The answer determines whether it can run alongside everything else.

---

## 8. Think It Through to About Seventy Percent, Then Open Several Sessions at Once

So what if several things on your plate are all still in the exploration stage? Do not force yourself to open several windows at once and end up fighting yourself; that will only get you confused faster. Your brain can only deeply think through one thing at a time, and forcing three exploratory tasks to run in parallel just means all three end up half-baked.

The right move is to go one after another, not all at once: spend a dedicated chunk of time on each item, say twenty minutes, and talk it through thoroughly with the AI until you reach **seventy percent maturity**: the overall direction is not going to change anymore, and you know what the first step and second step look like. That is the point where you stop. You do not need to think it through to one hundred percent before you start acting; that is far too slow, and a lot of the details genuinely only become visible once you actually start doing the work. No amount of pure thinking will surface them.

Once everything on your plate has been pushed to seventy percent, then you open several sessions at once, and each one advances its own plan by its first step. At this point your role has changed: you are no longer deeply thinking through several things simultaneously, you are switching back and forth, reviewing what each session hands back, and deciding what happens next. This is the real, actual working style behind people who single-handedly produce ten times the output: **go through exploration in sequence, run execution in parallel.**

## 9. This Feeling Is, Quite Literally, Being a Boss

Does this whole method feel familiar? Have you ever worked at a large company, or heard a friend complain about a scene like this: at the start of every quarter, the meeting rooms are booked solid back to back. First there is one big all-hands, dozens of people, team leads pulled in from every group, all sitting together, while the big boss stands up front and lays out where the company needs to push this quarter. He delivers it with real energy, but if you took a magnifying glass to this "direction," you would find it is nowhere near detailed enough to hand straight to someone as an execution plan: roughly what the outcome should look like, roughly what the priorities are, but the actual first step and second step, even the big boss up on stage cannot fully spell them out. He gets to a certain point and stops there.

This is not the big boss being lazy. Quite the opposite, this is exactly where the intelligence shows. He knows perfectly well: thinking something through to seventy or eighty percent is already the limit of what one person can nail down in the time available. The rest of the detail has to come from people who are closer to the actual ground-level work.

The moment the meeting breaks up is when the real show begins. Each team lead carries this seventy-percent direction back to their own turf, closes the door, and goes through it repeatedly with their team: what does this direction concretely mean for our group, what do we tackle first, who does it, how long will it take. By the end of this round, the seventy-percent direction has been hammered into a hundred-percent plan, concrete down to what a specific person needs to deliver on a specific day. Push one level further, down to the people actually doing the work, and what lands in their hands is a perfectly clear task card. No more guessing at direction, just execute it.

And from that point on, all the way to the end of the quarter, what the big boss and the team leads are actually spending their time on is, honestly, simple: checking in with people every few days, seeing whether progress is on track, and deciding whether some unforeseen surprise means the direction needs adjusting. They are no longer doing deep thinking. They are reviewing.

Now map this entire flow onto your own dealings with AI, and you will find it is identical. When you open a new session and go back and forth with the AI, one line at a time, turning something fuzzy into something you have a real feel for, but not yet detailed enough to execute step by step, the role you are playing in that moment is exactly the big boss up on stage laying out the direction. When you turn that seventy-percent direction into a plan document, open several sessions at once to push it forward on multiple fronts, and switch back and forth reviewing results, offering input, and calling the next move, the role you are playing in that moment is exactly the team lead running the daily check-in to align on progress.

This kind of skill used to be something only people who had actually led a team, who had actually managed people under them, ever got the chance to practice. If you had never sat in that seat, you simply had no way to get a feel for this kind of complexity: keeping several things in mind at once, knowing exactly where each one stands, being able to walk back in after a few days away and pick it right back up without your head turning into a mess. Now you do not have to wait until the day you actually become someone's manager. Right here in this course, every time you sit in front of several AI sessions switching back and forth, you are already quietly banking this exact experience. And the day you do end up leading a team for real, you will find that this muscle memory was already there, waiting for you.

## 10. Exercise

### Exercise: Run Two Things Forward at the Same Time

**Goal:** Walk through this entire way of working with your own hands, rather than just filing it away as something you understand in theory.

**How to do it:**

1. Pick two things you genuinely need to get done right now, work-related or study-related is fine, that you have not yet figured out how to approach.
2. Open a separate session for each one. Do not start executing yet. Spend ten or fifteen minutes talking through the goal and steps for each with the AI, and have it write the result into a plan document. Stop once you reach seventy percent maturity.
3. Once both have reached seventy percent, open two sessions at the same time, dispatch the first step for each, and switch back and forth reviewing results and deciding what comes next.

**What you should notice:**

You can genuinely keep more things moving forward at once than you ever could working through them one after another the old way, and you will not lose track of what you were doing the way you used to, because the plan document is always right there, ready for you to check back against at any time.

> **Key insight:** Go through exploration one at a time. Parallel execution only makes sense once exploration is done. If you cannot tell these two states apart, efficiency is not even on the table.

## 11. Recap, What We Learned

- AI needs time to work. Instead of zoning out while you wait, open several windows at once, hand off a task to each, and rotate through reviewing them.
- The human brain is not built for parallel memory, so write your plan before you start. A good notebook beats a good memory, and that is what lets you pick a session back up even after you have lost it.
- Tasks fall into two categories: exploratory tasks must happen in sequence, execution-and-review tasks can run in parallel.
- When several things are all still in the exploration stage, push each one to seventy percent maturity separately first, then open multiple sessions at once to execute them in parallel.
- This whole mindset is, at its core, team management: plan first, then dispatch work in parallel, then switch back and forth reviewing progress. Working with AI this way, you end up building this exact skill as a side effect.
